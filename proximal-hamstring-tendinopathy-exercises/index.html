Proximal Hamstring Tendinopathy Exercises

The upper hamstring tendon attaches to the bony point you sit on. Long periods on a hard chair, running uphill, and bending deeply at the hips can make it sore. This plan starts with a heel press on your back and gradually builds strength for those positions.

Before you start

Use this plan if you are an adult with clinician-diagnosed proximal hamstring tendinopathy. It is designed for gradual pain where the hamstring joins the sit bone. A sudden injury, diagnosed tear, or recent operation requires a separate plan.

This program may fit

You can point to a familiar ache low in one buttock near the sit bone. It came on gradually and is brought on by sitting, hills, faster running, or deep bending. You can walk without marked weakness.

Get assessed first

Arrange an exam for pain traveling below the knee, numbness or tingling, back pain with leg symptoms, or deep buttock pain at rest or at night. A teenager with pain after sprinting or kicking needs assessment for a growth-plate injury.

Stop signs

Get same-day assessment for sudden severe pain or a pop after a slip, split, kick, or sprint, especially with weakness, trouble walking, or bruising down the thigh. Bruising can appear later. Go to an emergency department now for new numbness around the genitals or anus, difficulty starting or emptying your bladder, loss of bladder or bowel control, or rapidly worsening leg weakness.

Check the next morning

Notice how long you can sit in your usual chair before the ache starts. Use the same chair and compare the same amount of time the day after exercise. You can also track how the first few minutes of walking feel.

Your home program

Build strength before increasing how far you bend

Begin with the heel press, then switch to moving bridges when comfortable. The low step-up is optional if stairs or hills matter to you. Save the hip hinge until moving hamstring exercises have gone well for at least two weeks and sitting in your usual chair feels the same the next day. Allow longer if the leg is still much weaker. Early on, break up long periods on hard seats and ease off long stretches, hills, or fast runs that aggravate the sit bone.

Frequency
Strength on 2 to 3 nonconsecutive days per week. Use comfortable level walks between sessions.
Equipment
Exercise mat or firm bed, a wall, and a low step with a stable rail or counter if you use the optional step-up
First checkpoint
Track sitting and walking each week. Building strength for running or deep bending can take several months.
Primary goal
Sit, walk uphill, and bend at the hips with less pain at the sit bone.
  1. 01
    Exercise 1

    Lying heel press

    2 to 3 nonconsecutive days per week as a starting option

    Dose3 holds of 15 to 30 seconds, resting 45 to 60 seconds between holds

    Lie on your back with both knees bent and heels on a firm surface. Keep your hips down. Press both heels down as if trying to drag them toward you without moving them. Aim to feel the backs of the thighs working and keep breathing.

    Make it easier

    Press more lightly or begin with 5- to 10-second holds.

    Progress it

    After several comfortable sessions, lift your hips an inch during a hold. Try the moving bridge when your sitting and walking feel usual the next day.

    My starting dose or notes

  2. 02
    Exercise 2

    Slow two-leg bridge

    2 to 3 nonconsecutive days per week after heel presses are comfortable

    Dose2 sets of 6 to 10 repetitions, with 1 to 2 minutes of rest

    Lie on your back with knees bent and feet hip-width apart. Push your heels down and raise your hips until your trunk and thighs form a comfortable line. Take two seconds to lift and three seconds to lower. Keep your pelvis level.

    Make it easier

    Lift a shorter distance, or return to heel presses if lying bridges make sitting worse later.

    Progress it

    Build to 12 smooth repetitions. Then move your heels an inch farther away or shift a little more weight to the sore leg. Change one thing at a time. When two-leg bridges feel easy, your clinician can help you progress to a single-leg bridge or add resistance.

    My starting dose or notes

  3. 03
    Exercise 3

    Supported low step-up

    Optional, 2 to 3 nonconsecutive days per week once bridges are manageable, if stairs or hills are a goal.

    Dose2 sets of 6 to 10 repetitions on each side, with about 1 minute of rest

    Use a secure low step with a handrail or counter alongside. Place the whole working foot on the step. Stand tall as you press through that foot, touch the other foot to the step, then lower it slowly to the floor. Finish one side before switching.

    Make it easier

    Choose a lower step or use more hand support.

    Progress it

    Build repetitions on the low step first. Save a higher step that bends the hip farther until moving hamstring exercises have gone well for at least two weeks, next-day sitting is stable, and your leg no longer feels markedly weak.

    My starting dose or notes

  4. 04
    Exercise 4

    Shallow hip hinge

    2 nonconsecutive days per week, once ready for the hinge.

    DoseStart with 2 sets of 4 to 6 repetitions, resting about 1 minute between sets

    Start after at least two weeks of well-tolerated moving hamstring strength work. Wait longer if the leg is still much weaker or sitting in your usual chair feels worse the next day. Stand a few inches in front of a wall with your back toward it, feet hip-width apart. Soften the knees and send the hips back toward the wall as your trunk tips forward a little. Stand tall again. Start shallow and stop before a strong pull at the sit bone.

    Make it easier

    Stand nearer the wall and shorten the bend. Delay this move if the sit bone still hurts more the next day.

    Progress it

    After several sessions with no extra pain the next day, deepen the hinge a little. Once the bend you need is comfortable, try a light weight held close. If you stopped running completely, ask your clinician about restarting. They can assess your strength and how you tolerate three sessions of loading with the hip bent, plus a 30- to 40-minute walk with pain no higher than 2 out of 10 and no increase 24 hours later.

    My starting dose or notes

Symptom response

Let the next day guide the dose.

Notice how long you can sit in your usual chair before the ache starts. Use the same chair and compare the same amount of time the day after exercise. You can also track how the first few minutes of walking feel.

Continue

Green light

You feel only a mild, familiar ache that stays steady. Movement remains smooth. The next day, you can sit or walk about as long as usual without extra pain.

Adjust

Yellow light

If pain increases through a set or your usual chair is clearly less comfortable the next day, shorten the exercise range, reduce repetitions, or return to the previous exercise. Restart at the last dose your daily activities tolerated.

Stop

Red light

Stop exercising. Get same-day assessment for sudden severe pain or a pop after a slip, split, kick, or sprint, especially with weakness, trouble walking, or bruising down the thigh. Bruising can appear later. Go to an emergency department now for new numbness around the genitals or anus, difficulty starting or emptying your bladder, loss of bladder or bowel control, or rapidly worsening leg weakness.

Change one variable at a time

When symptoms are too reactive, first reduce range, resistance, repetitions, or frequency. When the current dose feels controlled for several sessions, progress only one of those variables.

Progression

Build capacity in stages.

  1. Settle pressure from sitting

    Try a pressure-relieving cushion or change chairs. Stand up before the ache builds on longer trips or at your desk. Use heel presses and tolerable level walking while reducing deep bends and hills.

  2. Restore strength on two legs

    Switch from heel presses to moving bridges. When two-leg bridges feel easy, your clinician can help you load the hamstring more heavily. Add the optional low step-up if you want to practice stairs or hills. Change one exercise at a time and check sitting and walking the next day.

  3. Practice the bend you need

    Start with a shallow hinge after at least two weeks of moving hamstring exercises that leave sitting in your usual chair no worse the next day. Allow longer if the leg is still much weaker. Deepen the bend a little at a time toward a task you need, such as picking up a light object.

  4. Return to faster activity if needed

    If you stopped running completely, plan your first walk-jog with your clinician. Check that you can complete three sessions of strength work with the hip bent without a lasting flare. Also check that you can walk 30 to 40 minutes with pain of 2 out of 10 or less and no increase 24 hours later. Save speed, long strides, and steep hills for later.

Signs you are ready for the next stage

  • The time you can spend in your usual chair is stable or increasing across the week.
  • Before starting the hip hinge, you have tolerated at least two weeks of moving hamstring strength work. Sitting in the same chair feels usual the next day. Allow longer if the leg is still much weaker.
  • You can reach the depth of bend needed for your daily tasks without sharp pain or a next-day flare.
  • If you stopped running completely, review your readiness with your clinician. Check how you handle strength work with the hip bent and a 30- to 40-minute walk with pain no higher than 2 out of 10 and no increase 24 hours later.

When to schedule an evaluation

Arrange follow-up for worsening sitting tolerance, spreading leg pain, or little improvement after several weeks. Get same-day assessment for sudden severe pain or a pop after a slip, split, kick, or sprint, especially with weakness, trouble walking, or bruising down the thigh. Bruising can appear later. Go to an emergency department now for new numbness around the genitals or anus, difficulty starting or emptying your bladder, loss of bladder or bowel control, or rapidly worsening leg weakness.

Questions about this program

How can I make sitting easier?

Try a cushion that takes pressure off the sore spot, adjust the seat angle, or alternate sitting with standing. Take a short break before the usual onset of pain. Gradually lengthen sitting time as it becomes comfortable.

Should I stretch the back of my thigh?

A long stretch with a straight knee and deeply bent hip can press the sore tendon against the sit bone. Use a comfortable range early on. Before trying a hip hinge, allow at least two weeks of moving hamstring work with stable sitting on the same chair the next day. Allow longer if the leg is still much weaker.

Do I have to stop all walking or running?

Keep comfortable level walks. If you still run, reduce hills, speed, or distance if they bring on pain. If even an easy run makes you limp or worsens sitting the next day, switch to an activity you tolerate. If you stopped running completely, review a restart with your clinician after you tolerate strength work with the hip bent and a 30- to 40-minute walk with pain of 2 out of 10 or less and no increase 24 hours later.

Is one treatment proven best?

A 100-person trial compared individualized physiotherapy with shockwave therapy. Both groups also received education. The trial found no significant difference in the main measures of function or overall improvement. Your clinician can adapt exercise to the activities you want to regain.

When should I get checked?

Arrange follow-up for worsening sitting tolerance, spreading leg pain, or little improvement after several weeks. Get same-day assessment for sudden severe pain or a pop after a slip, split, kick, or sprint, especially with weakness, trouble walking, or bruising down the thigh. Bruising can appear later. Go to an emergency department now for new numbness around the genitals or anus, difficulty starting or emptying your bladder, loss of bladder or bowel control, or rapidly worsening leg weakness.

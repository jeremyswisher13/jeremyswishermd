Before you start

Use this starter plan if you are an adult and your clinician has diagnosed insertional Achilles tendinopathy. Follow your treating team's separate instructions after a tear, fracture, or operation.

This program may fit

The tender spot is at the back of the heel, within about 2 cm (three-quarters of an inch) of the tendon attachment. Symptoms came on gradually. The first steps after rest, a shoe pressing on the heel, or an uphill walk may bring on familiar pain.

Get assessed first

Have the location checked if the main pain is underneath the heel, farther up the tendon, or deep in the bone. Arrange follow-up if pain at rest is increasing, swelling around the heel is getting worse, walking is getting harder, or several weeks pass with little improvement.

Stop signs

Get same-day care for a sudden pop or kick-like feeling behind the ankle, loss of push-off, or inability to bear weight after injury. A tear can still allow some walking. Get same-day care for new unexplained swelling in one calf, or calf pain or tenderness with warmth or a change in skin color. Seek care right away if redness spreads quickly, even without fever, or if red, painful skin comes with fever or chills. Call 911 for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or fainting.

Check the next morning

Notice heel pain during exercise, an hour later, and with your first steps the next morning. Note how long the morning stiffness lasts. Before increasing the load, compare your average pain and stiffness with the previous week.

Your home program

Raise the heel from a flat floor

Choose one calf exercise per strength workout. In a guided session, skip the other two calf exercises. Start with a seated hold or raise you can control. As strength improves, switch to the next option at a later workout. Use a flat floor and skip off-step heel drops and deep calf stretches while the attachment is sore. Add a comfortable amount of level walking.

Frequency
Strength on 2 to 3 nonconsecutive days per week. Walk on days your heel tolerates it.
Equipment
Firm chair, stable counter, flat floor, and comfortable walking shoes
First checkpoint
Each week, compare your average heel pain, how long morning stiffness lasts, and how far you can walk. Hold off on increases if pain or stiffness is getting worse. Allow at least 12 weeks to build strength.
Primary goal
Walk with a comfortable push-off and rebuild calf strength for stairs or hills.
  1. 01
    Exercise 1

    Seated heel lift and hold

    Choose only one calf exercise per strength workout, 2 to 3 nonconsecutive days per week.

    Dose3 holds of 15 to 30 seconds, with 45 to 60 seconds of rest

    Use this hold if moving raises hurt. Sit on a firm chair with both feet flat and knees bent. Press the balls of your feet into the floor. Lift your heels a little, hold steady, then lower gently. Breathe throughout.

    Make it easier

    Use a lower lift or hold for 5 to 10 seconds.

    Progress it

    Build toward the longer hold. If the heel feels usual an hour later and the next morning and the week is going well, try seated raises in place of holds at a later workout.

    My starting dose or notes

  2. 02
    Exercise 2

    Slow seated calf raise

    Choose only one calf exercise per strength workout, 2 to 3 nonconsecutive days per week.

    Dose2 sets of 8 to 12, with about 1 minute of rest

    Use seated raises when you can lift and lower comfortably. Sit tall, knees bent and feet under the knees. Keep your toes relaxed. Take about two seconds to lift both heels and three seconds to return them to the floor.

    Make it easier

    Start with one set, or use a smaller motion.

    Progress it

    Press down lightly on your thighs for resistance. When two sets feel easy, the heel feels usual an hour later and the next morning, and pain and stiffness are steady across the week, switch to standing raises at a later workout.

    My starting dose or notes

  3. 03
    Exercise 3

    Supported standing calf raise

    Choose only one calf exercise per strength workout, 2 to 3 nonconsecutive days per week.

    Dose2 sets of 6 to 10, with 1 to 2 minutes of rest

    Choose standing raises once seated raises feel easy. Stand facing a stable counter with your feet hip-width apart on the floor. Hold the counter. Keep knees straight but relaxed. Rise evenly on both forefeet, pause, and lower slowly until both heels touch the floor.

    Make it easier

    Take more weight through your hands or use the seated calf raise.

    Progress it

    Build to 12 even raises per set. Shift more weight to the sore leg over several sessions, then try a supported one-leg raise. Once that feels easy, ask your clinician how to add resistance gradually on level ground.

    My starting dose or notes

  4. 04
    Exercise 4

    Level walking

    Most days if heel pain stays mild an hour later and first steps feel usual the next morning.

    DoseOne easy walk, starting below the time that usually increases heel pain

    Pick an even route close to home and wear shoes that let the heel move comfortably. Five minutes is a possible starting point if that already feels easy. Use a natural stride and turn back before pain changes how you walk.

    Make it easier

    Shorten the route or split it into two brief walks with a break.

    Progress it

    After several comfortable walks with steady or improving pain and stiffness across the week, extend the time by a few minutes. Keep the route flat while testing the longer walk. Add inclines only after level walking stays comfortable.

    My starting dose or notes

Symptom response

Let the next day guide the dose.

Notice heel pain during exercise, an hour later, and with your first steps the next morning. Note how long the morning stiffness lasts. Before increasing the load, compare your average pain and stiffness with the previous week.

Continue

Green light

A familiar ache stays mild during exercise and an hour later. You walk normally. First-step pain and morning stiffness return to their usual level. If these checks hold for several sessions and pain and stiffness are steady or improving week to week, try one small increase.

Adjust

Yellow light

Hold off on increasing if pain builds, you limp, your heel aches more an hour later, or the next morning is worse. If pain or stiffness is increasing from week to week, return to the last comfortable dose or walking time. Shorten the motion if the heel feels pinched.

Stop

Red light

Stop exercising. Get same-day care for a sudden pop or kick-like feeling behind the ankle, loss of push-off, or inability to bear weight after injury. A tear can still allow some walking. Get same-day care for new unexplained swelling in one calf, or calf pain or tenderness with warmth or a change in skin color. Seek care right away if redness spreads quickly, even without fever, or if red, painful skin comes with fever or chills. Call 911 for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or fainting.

Change one variable at a time

When symptoms are too reactive, first reduce range, resistance, repetitions, or frequency. When the current dose feels controlled for several sessions, progress only one of those variables.

Progression

Build capacity in stages.

  1. Find a comfortable starting load

    Use one seated calf exercise and a tolerable level walk. Temporarily shorten steep routes. If the back of a shoe rubs, try a comfortable pair that relieves that pressure.

  2. Move from seated to standing

    When seated raises feel easy for several sessions, check the heel an hour later and on the next morning's first steps. If both feel usual and pain and stiffness are steady or improving across the week, switch to supported standing raises. Build repetitions before shifting weight to one leg.

  3. Rebuild the walking you need

    Once level walking and repeated standing raises feel comfortable, try a short, gentle incline at your usual pace. When supported one-leg raises feel easy, work with your clinician to increase resistance gradually. Discuss running or jumping before adding impact.

Signs you are ready for the next stage

  • Pain stays mild an hour after several workouts. First steps feel usual the next morning, and average pain and stiffness are steady or improving from week to week.
  • You can lower each heel smoothly to the floor without a pinch or loss of control.
  • A level walk at your usual pace leaves your stride comfortable that day and the next.
  • Before adding hills, you can do repeated standing raises with steady height and comfortable push-off.

When to schedule an evaluation

Arrange follow-up for increasing pain at rest, worsening heel swelling, more difficulty walking, or little improvement after several weeks. Get same-day care for a sudden pop or kick-like feeling behind the ankle, loss of push-off, or inability to bear weight after injury. A tear can still allow some walking. Get same-day care for new unexplained swelling in one calf, or calf pain or tenderness with warmth or a change in skin color. Seek care right away if redness spreads quickly, even without fever, or if red, painful skin comes with fever or chills. Call 911 for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or fainting.

Common questions

Questions about this program

Why keep my heels on floor level at the bottom?

When the heel drops below the forefoot, the tendon bends more sharply around the heel bone. Starting on the floor lets the calf work while limiting pressure at the sore attachment.

Would a heel lift help?

Ask your clinician if a temporary lift fits your shoes and walking pattern. A trial in 42 sport-active adults with longstanding insertional pain found better outcomes at 12 and 24 weeks with a program combining heel lifts, limited ankle bend, and progressive exercise. The trial tested those changes together.

Should I stretch my calf?

Skip prolonged wall stretches and off-step heel drops during the sore early stage. If ankle motion later limits an activity you need, your clinician can help you reintroduce it gradually.

Can I start running again?

First build comfortable level walking and repeated calf raises. A clinician can check strength and how you tolerate impact, then help you choose a starting walk-jog dose. A better day alone does not establish readiness.
